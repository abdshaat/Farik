# Estimating landed cost

The landed cost is what one unit costs once it has arrived: the number the founder compares, not
the price on the listing. It matters most for goods that cross a border, such as stock bought from
an overseas maker, and for heavy goods, such as a pallet of tiles. A service has no landed cost.

## 1. The parts

Add these, for the whole order, then divide by the number of units:
- the price of the goods;
- shipping to the address the founder gave, by the way the seller offers;
- insurance, if the seller or the carrier charges for it;
- import duty, which depends on what the goods are and where they come from;
- tax, such as sales tax or value added tax, and whether the business can reclaim it;
- fees at the border or on delivery, such as a broker's or a carrier's handling fee;
- the cost of changing money, if the seller is paid in another currency.

## 2. One currency, and one number

Put every part in the founder's currency, converting as `comparing-offers` says. End with one
number for each unit, delivered, and show the parts that make it.

## 3. Say which figure is a guess

A seller's price and shipping come from its page. Duty, tax and fees often do not: say for each
figure whether it is the seller's, an agency's published rate or your estimate, and where the
founder can confirm it, such as the customs agency's tariff page or the carrier's rate page. Never
present an estimate as a quote. If a figure could change the winner, say so.
