## The pain point (from the research)

AI can now write code and build a product. The work around the product is where people get stuck:

1. **Getting users.** Writers say building got cheap and attention became the limit; "build it and they will come" is named as a top killer, and marketing is an ongoing system, not a launch day.
2. **Keeping it good.** Reviews of the prompt-to-app tools (Lovable, Bolt, Replit, v0) say each still needs manual work before real use. Complaints cluster around credit costs, brittle changes, regressions and security gaps.
3. **Testing with real people.** AI feedback on a design is broad and shallow, and one vendor study had AI agents succeed far less often than human testers. The consensus is that AI helps with repeatable checks and real users are still needed.
4. **Trust.** Writers on agent governance say a named human must stay answerable, autonomy should grow step by step, and an audit trail matters.

## The competition (from the research)

- **Polsia**: an AI operations platform where agents do research, landing pages, ads and outreach. It leans towards autonomy, and a hands-on review warns a connected ad budget can be spent by its growth agent.
- **Devin**: one autonomous AI software engineer.
- **CrewAI and MetaGPT**: open-source frameworks for developers to build agent teams.
- **Cursor and coding agents**: help inside the code editor.
- **Lovable, Bolt, Replit, v0**: prompt-to-app builders aimed at the first version.

In what I found, builders stop at the first version, frameworks need a developer, and the run-the-business platforms lean on autonomy. Control for a non-technical owner is the angle I did not find taken. This is one research session, not proof that no one else is there.

## The promise

**An AI team that runs the work around your product, and you stay in charge.**

What the product plan lets us say behind it:

- A team of 2 to 7 named agents, each with a role. The roles in the plan include Product Manager, Architect, Developer, Marketing Specialist, Scrum Master and UI/UX Designer, so planning, architecture, building and marketing each have an owner.
- Every task has a written contract and exit criteria before work starts, and nobody accepts their own work.
- A rule-checker written in code (the "governor") checks every permission, budget, iteration limit and path. Agents propose; the governor decides.
- Outward acts, such as spending money or posting, need your approval or an approved plan. This plan is itself an example.
- Every action goes into an append-only log, with its cost.
- A non-technical person sets up and runs the team in the web app, with no terminal.
- Local first, on your own key. Sessions are bounded, and hitting a limit asks you.

## What we do not say

- We do not say you can download or use Catervas today. The roadmap says there is no release; the web launch (`v0.1.0`, open source) is phase 13.
- We do not claim the team does user testing. The product plan names no such ability. Its own tests use real people (5 test users, at least 3 non-technical). We say plainly that real users still test, and we do not say AI replaces them.
- We do not say "fully managed" or "hosted". The product plan says local first; paid cloud-hosted workspaces are phase 17, the last. Posts use "a team you run", not "a managed service". If you mean the hosted service, tell me and I will plan it once it has a date.
- "All control" in posts means the specific controls listed above, not a blanket guarantee.
- We do not name competitors in posts or compare prices. Their prices are unverified.
- We do not cite the roadmap's estimate of about $25 for a first sprint day: the spec calls it an estimate, not a cap.
